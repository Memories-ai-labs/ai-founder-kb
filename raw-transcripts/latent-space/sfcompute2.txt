Origin: SF Compute began by trying to sublease a GPU cluster. Serving customers' needs pushed it to build most of the operating capacity of a cloud.

Physical settlement: Buyers receive usable compute, not just a cash payment against an index. SF Compute co-designed physical settlement and its order book, so real constraints like interruptibility, storage recovery, and hardware generation shape both contracts and prices.

Order book design: Rather than waiting for standardized contracts, orders are matched on multiple attributes in a matching engine. This generates liquidity before the technology settles, at the cost of more bid-ask spread.

Liquidity: About 5% of capacity is on the order book. Success measured by whether it solves customers' problems.

Pricing: Forward curve with a hump rather than a simple slope. Large gap between inference revenue per B300 hour and the cost to buy one — called a "huge arbitrage."

Independence: SF Compute doesn't own hardware, which keeps it neutral enough for others to trade on. NVIDIA and large cloud/inference providers have structural conflicts that prevent them from building a neutral exchange.

Cash vs. physical settlement: Cash-settled derivatives are useful but should derive from physical settlement. Indexes built on thin data can be manipulated — parallel to LIBOR.

Ecosystem: Silicon Data as an early player taking a cash-settlement approach; emerging companies working on standards, residual value insurance, and financing.

Recent product: Customers can subdivide InfiniBand fabrics on the fly without contacting SF Compute — virtualizing the data center.
