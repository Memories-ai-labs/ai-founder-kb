TOPICS AND TIMESTAMPS:

[00:00:00] How Caro gets people to talk
He pursues essential witnesses even when they refuse. John Connally eventually explained how Johnson used money to build political power.

[00:05:03] Moses and building today
Caro argues Moses succeeded despite safeguards and opposition. A figure with that drive could emerge again. He doesn't want someone as ruthless as Moses.

[00:12:42] Moses's change over time
Moses started as an idealist focused on public beaches and parks, then hardened into someone who overrode opposition — as with the Cross Bronx Expressway.

[00:17:57] Moses losing power
Caro watched Moses's influence fade during the writing of The Power Broker. He describes it as tragic.

[00:27:53] Building in the Depression
The Depression gave the city access to cheap, plentiful labor, which helped Moses build quickly.

[00:32:51] Johnson's power
Johnson's persuasion rested on his ability to grant or deny favors — committee assignments and legislation.

[00:36:08] Kennedy vs. Johnson
Caro argues Kennedy lacked Johnson's drive to change America domestically. Vietnam undermined Johnson's domestic agenda.

[00:41:00] Writing process
Caro reduces each book to a one-sentence thesis before writing. The research archive includes much material that never made the books.

[00:52:59] Loneliness
Caro discusses the isolation of frontier life in the Hill Country and how his own solitary work routine has affected his relationships.

KEY INSIGHTS:
- Power is built through controlling specific mechanisms (favors, committee seats, bond contracts, legislation) not through charisma alone
- Moses entrenched his power by drafting laws that gave his public authorities expansive, long-lasting powers tied to bond contracts
- Moses started idealistic and hardened; Johnson started opportunistic and used power for civil rights later
- Reducing research to a one-sentence thesis before writing helps navigate massive archival material
