# Responding to objections to recursive self-improvement
# URL: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/rsiobjections
# Date: 2026-11-24
# source: Dwarkesh Podcast

Guest: Dwarkesh Patel (solo episode — responding to objections on recursive self-improvement / RSI)
Max Farrens (co-discussant)

---

**Setup:** Dwarkesh Patel and Max Farrens respond to objections to Patel's essay on recursive self-improvement (RSI). Patel's median estimate is that AI can fully automate top AI research around 2028, with superintelligence about a year later. He gives roughly a 25% chance of superintelligence next year.

**Key objections and responses:**

Is AI research verifiable? Patel argues it has many measurable proxies — pretraining loss, how fast a model learns a new video game, open math problems solved. Gains in one area tend to show up across many.

Where does data progress come from? A study Patel ran with a Princeton student suggests that improved data filtering and synthetic data explain much of pretraining progress. ML researchers can do this work without deep domain expertise.

Generalization: Models often succeed on tasks they weren't specifically trained for, as with math problems and a social deduction game benchmark.

Sample efficiency and continual learning: Models already learn quickly within a context window. The open question is whether that ability can extend over longer periods.

Evolution as evidence: Patel argues that evolution's optimization was far larger than current AI training, and that transfer from it is a reasonable analogy, though not proof.

Paul Christiano's credibility: Patel points to Christiano's earlier predictions — forecasts about AI architecture and reward-seeking behavior — as reasons to take his views seriously. He cites the "Hugging Face" incident as an example of reward hacking.

How would AI take over? Patel admits there's no knockdown argument. He uses historical analogies (the conquest of the Aztecs) to argue that the stakes warrant serious attention to AI motivations.

Motivations and alignment: Patel argues that reward-based training may produce increasingly misaligned systems. Two counterpoints: misaligned AI might prevent full automation of research, and early "warning shots" would likely appear before a catastrophe.

Many models checking each other: Patel is concerned that AIs from one lineage may be too similar to constrain each other, and that they could coordinate easily.

METR data and acceleration: METR reported that Anthropic's progress on well-scoped tasks is about twice as fast as a year earlier. Patel cites other signals including rising AI spending by OpenAI researchers, self-reported productivity gains, and lab usage figures. He acknowledges these are circumstantial and that labs withhold much of the relevant data.

Is it recursive if humans are in the loop? Patel agrees it isn't fully recursive yet. He argues that speedups grow faster than linearly as more tasks are automated, so partial automation still matters a lot.

Math analogy: Patel concedes AI research may not speed up as dramatically as math did, since experiments are costly. He argues that even a tenth of math's pace would compress a large amount of ML progress into one year.

Compute bottlenecks: Experiment compute is a real constraint, but AI systems working around the clock at high speed could still substantially accelerate research.

**Outlook:** Patel expects colleague-like AI agents that can own projects by end of next year. He expects the intelligence explosion to come later, around 2028–2029. Internal lab systems may be well ahead of public ones. His closing advice: weigh the full set of arguments and evidence, stay skeptical without dismissing the possibility.
