## Overview

Dwarkesh Patel presents a thought experiment on AI lab economics: if Anthropic sustains 10x annual revenue growth while compute only 3x-es per year, something must give — margins, prices, or allocation.

## Main Thesis

"If a true human-level software engineer that could run on an H100 equivalent, at current market rates for software engineers, that H100 should rent for over $250k a year. That's 15x today's spot prices."

## Three Mechanisms to Reconcile Revenue/Compute Growth Disparity

1. Lab margins increase to mid-90s percent range (from today's ~50-60%)
2. Compute prices rise substantially (15x is a plausible ceiling)
3. Greater compute allocation toward inference vs. training (as models mature)

## Key Market Signal

Google pays SpaceX approximately $900 million monthly for 110,000 GPUs — roughly double spot prices and 40% above February 2026 baseline rates. This suggests top-tier buyers are already paying 2x market for guaranteed allocation.

## Structural Supply Constraints (Through 2030)

- EUV lithography tool bottlenecks (ASML is the primary constraint)
- AI wafer allocation saturation reaching ~86% by end of 2027
- Data center power availability constraints (grid capacity vs. demand)
- These constraints are largely inelastic to demand signals

## Implications for Founders

1. **Superior models command premium pricing** — if compute costs 10x more, only high-value applications survive; commoditized AI use cases get squeezed first
2. **Inefficient applications face existential pricing pressure** — any application burning 10x tokens to do something that could be done efficiently will lose margin headroom
3. **Leading labs' competitive moats strengthen** — labs with better inference efficiency and better model quality (same output, less compute) win disproportionately as margins tighten
4. **Infrastructure startups with long-term compute contracts gain edge** — similar to energy hedging strategies

## Strategic Implication for AI Startups

If compute gets 10x more expensive, the application layer needs 10x better monetization per unit of compute consumed. This is a filter for quality: startups solving problems with clear willingness-to-pay survive; "interesting demos" do not.

The bet that "compute gets cheaper over time" may be wrong in the short-to-medium term (2026-2030), even if it holds long-term as fabrication capacity catches up.
