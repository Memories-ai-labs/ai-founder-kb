# Alex Imas and Phil Trammell – What remains scarce after AGI?
# URL: https://www.dwarkesh.com/p/alex-imas-phil-trammell
# Date: 2026-06-04
# Source: Dwarkesh Podcast

**Guests:** Alex Imas (Director of AGI Economics at Google DeepMind, Professor at University of Chicago) and Phil Trammell (Head of Economics at Epoch, Research Scholar at Stanford)
**Host:** Dwarkesh Patel

---

## Key Discussion Points

### Labor Share and Automation

The conversation opens with fundamental questions about whether labor's share of economic output will decline as AI becomes more capable. Imas emphasizes historical caution: economists have made poor predictions about automation's effects since David Ricardo in 1820. Despite centuries of technological change, labor share has remained remarkably stable at approximately 60% of total economic output—a phenomenon some call a "Kaldor fact."

Both guests stress that "individual forecasts from economists aren't necessarily useful" and advocate instead for prediction markets and scenario planning rather than point predictions.

### What Will Remain Scarce?

Imas introduces the concept of the "relational sector"—services where human involvement is intrinsically valued. A human barista or therapist commands premium prices precisely because consumers prefer human interaction. However, this sector's size depends critically on unknowable variables: consumer willingness to pay for human involvement in different services.

Trammell uses a historical thought experiment about 15th-century Mongols trying to predict future scarcity. Just as they couldn't anticipate new categories of goods beyond songs and horse transportation, contemporary economists struggle predicting what forms of scarcity will emerge with transformative AI.

### The "Messy Middle" Scenario

The hosts discuss whether a plausible "messy middle" exists where AI automates jobs faster than wealth creation, making broad redistribution politically difficult without technological abundance justifying the displacement.

Imas argues several unlikely conditions must all hold simultaneously for this scenario: automation must be narrowly targeted (affecting only specific professions), yet not unlock broader capabilities; wealth creation must lag job displacement; and political economy must prevent rapid redistribution mechanisms.

The phone operator case (1920-1940) illustrates how automation can unfold gradually despite available technology, creating sustained political pressure rather than crisis-driven responses.

### Taxation and Redistribution

When discussing wealth redistribution from AI gains, Imas expresses concerns about UBI's political vulnerability compared to property-based approaches. "When you're dependent on a check, it matters who's in power," he notes, suggesting universal basic capital (equity ownership) provides more stable insurance.

Trammell proposes consumption taxes funding government purchase of diversified corporate equity—avoiding the targeting problem of identifying which specific companies will dominate AI's future.

### Why Automation Hasn't Caused Mass Unemployment Yet

Yale's Budget Lab finds minimal evidence of white-collar automation so far. Even software engineers, the most exposed sector, show continued aggregate demand growth despite some reduced hiring of junior developers.

Imas identifies a potential coordination problem: "public coordination devices" could create cascading layoffs as firms perform automation for signaling purposes rather than genuine efficiency gains, despite hurting themselves.

The O-ring theory of economic development explains persistent human involvement: when integrated production requires multiple steps, one unreliable automated component can ruin the entire output, making quality human oversight valuable despite partial automation.

### AI Preferences and Future Wealth Distribution

The discussion turns speculative: will future AIs and AI-directed entities value human-intrinsic goods? Imas argues evolved human preferences for empathy and human connection may persist through sexual selection if reproduction remains biological. Trammell counters that entities focused purely on wealth accumulation—whether out of intrinsic preference or instrumental reasons (philosophical influence, utilitarian cause expansion)—would naturally dominate economically through higher savings rates.

The key insight: "every robot now turns into many robots next year, but the number of ballerinas is the same." If some agents never satiate in capital demand while marginal utility of robot services declines, capital's share of the economy could approach 100% despite overall abundance.

### Developing Country Strategy

For nations outside AI's core production chain, the guests recommend prioritizing equity indexation over retraining. Buying diversified stakes in global AI companies via index funds requires less institutional capacity than retrain-and-hope strategies. However, they note the rise of private equity concentration and closed-source model advantages complicates indexation.

They express cautious optimism that open-source AI remaining 6-9 months behind frontier models democratizes access, similar to electricity's broad diffusion benefits relative to social media's concentrated gains.

### Open vs. Closed AI Models

Imas highlights the electricity versus social media distinction: electricity provided broad-based benefits despite centralized provision, while social media concentrated rents despite universal access. Whether frontier AI resembles electricity (everyone benefits through diverse applications) or social media (rents concentrate among platform operators) remains crucial for inequality outcomes.

Commodification risks include safety concerns from competitive pressure, but offers political benefits: distributed ownership of public companies prevents concentrated targets for government intervention, unlike OpenAI and Anthropic as standalone entities.

---

## Core Tensions

The episode identifies several unresolved tensions:

1. **Demand elasticity uncertainty**: Will people endlessly demand more software, medical advances, and varieties of automated goods, or will satiation occur?

2. **Indexation difficulty**: Can ordinary citizens adequately diversify into AI-adjacent companies when returns concentrate in private firms?

3. **Preference evolution**: Will future entities (human or artificial) maintain interest in relational goods, or will wealth accumulation drive everything?

4. **Timeline effects**: Do developing countries have time to index globally, or will transformative change occur too rapidly?

The hosts conclude that robust data collection on consumer demand elasticities, relational good preferences, and sectoral automation rates should precede major policy commitments, since economic theory permits multiple plausible futures depending on empirical parameters currently unknown.
