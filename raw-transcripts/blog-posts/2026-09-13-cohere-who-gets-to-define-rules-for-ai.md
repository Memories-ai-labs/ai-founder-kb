# Who Gets to Define the Rules for AI?
# Author: Aidan Gomez (Co-Founder & CEO, Cohere)
# URL: https://cohere.com/blog/who-gets-to-define-the-rules-for-ai
# Date: 2026-09-13
# Source: Cohere Blog
# Reading time: ~15 min

---

Artificial intelligence is fundamentally reshaping society. Within a generation, how we discover medicine, manage power grids, and secure infrastructure will be completely transformed. While many recognize this and work toward responsible development, others leverage these concerns to advance self-serving agendas.

The central question remains unasked: "Should a handful of market-dominant Silicon Valley AI companies define safety standards and regulations for the entire world?" Gomez warns this mirrors problematic historical patterns. Using fear as justification, these oligopolies now seek competition exemptions while dictating terms for everyone else—a cartel disguised as safety advocacy.

## Historical Parallels

The essay draws parallels to past regulatory failures. In 1975, the Securities and Exchange Commission designated three bond-rating firms as gatekeepers, creating a protected market. These firms later rated subprime securities triple-A, nearly collapsing the global economy.

Similarly, the European Union's 1985 Motor Vehicle Block Exemption allowed manufacturers to set safety standards for servicing, ostensibly protecting consumers. Instead, it took twenty-five years to unwind the resulting monopolistic protections while maintaining actual safety standards.

## The Current Proposal

Recent proposals from major AI labs seek antitrust exemptions under the guise of safety coordination. While independent AI system review has merit, critical concerns emerge regarding standard-setting authority, review oversight, and participation opportunities.

The plan would have "a handful of powerful labs agree on shared standards" while requesting governmental antitrust waivers enabling this coordination, then requiring all other developers to follow predetermined rules without input—a fundamentally undemocratic approach.

## Four Pillars for Better Governance

Gomez proposes evidence-based alternatives:

**1. Evidence-based risk framework:** Establish published accounts of specific harms, which capabilities cause them, and contextual conditions requiring intervention. Build this collaboratively across nations, openly rather than behind national security claims. Include technologists, policymakers, and sector experts—including those with differing perspectives.

**2. Mandatory transparency:** Developers should disclose how systems are built, intended purposes, capabilities, risks, and mitigation measures. Incident reporting mechanisms should ensure lessons benefit the entire field rather than remaining private.

**3. Scoped testing:** Independent testing should target genuine dangers identified through evidence—cyberattacks, synthetic fraud, voice cloning, manipulation, weapons development, and critical infrastructure threats. Testing should proportionally scale with capability level and deployment context, not developer resources.

**4. Real assurance mechanisms:** Adopt layered approaches similar to financial, aviation, and nuclear industries. Testing standards must be collectively developed and published. Third-party evaluators require diverse mandates, never financial conflicts with reviewed parties, and public accountability.

## Addressing Existential Anxiety

Recent warnings about superintelligence causing human extinction within a decade reflect genuine concerns but lack empirical foundations. As systems improve, the gap between intended and actual outcomes widens—a legitimate issue requiring serious safeguards.

However, "claiming such problems make tools uncontrollable represents judgment rather than established fact." This distinction separates scientific analysis from speculation. Open democratic processes should address such uncertainties, never by granting particular viewpoints binding regulatory authority.

Recent incidents occurred within the world's best-resourced labs with largest safety teams. The proposed solutions essentially replicate existing arrangements that already failed. What would actually help: mandatory incident reporting, testing against known vulnerabilities, stronger test-time observability, and physical isolation for critical infrastructure deployment.

## Practical Immediate Needs

Current harms demand attention: voice cloning tools costing less than phone service can deplete pensioners' accounts. Automated decision systems directly impact access to essential services today. A safety regime must address these existing threats rather than hypothetical superintelligences.

## The Core Issue: Democratic Legitimacy

The decisions made over coming months will shape the global economy generationally. Critical infrastructure cannot be secured by renting capability from foreign monopolies behind closed interfaces. Hospitals, payment networks, and defense ministries cannot ethically route sensitive operational data to external servers while trusting vendor contracts for protection.

Cohere was built precisely addressing this reality. Global governments need highly capable systems operating within their own controlled infrastructure, from replaceable providers answering to them. Security emerges from sovereignty, local deployment, and technological diversity.

"More voices makes it slower and harder, and some will say unwelcome things—that's precisely the point." Only this approach produces rulesets the public has legitimate reason to trust.

A credible process includes academics without commercial stakes, civil society skeptical of industry speed, smaller labs, open-source developers, and governments with independent verification needs. If this technology genuinely remakes our world, a handful of CEOs cannot unilaterally determine evolution.

## Cohere's Commitment

Companies can either discuss responsible paces and external oversight, or simply build responsibly at sustainable speeds, enable partner sovereignty, and collaborate with lawmakers and safety experts. Cohere chooses the latter approach.
